Everybody knows what a black hole does. It sucks. It reaches out across space, grabs whatever drifts too close, and hauls it in.

Everybody is wrong. And the real answer is stranger.

Here's a thought experiment. Swap out the Sun. Take it away, and put in its place a black hole with exactly the same mass. Nothing else changes.

Earth would keep orbiting. Same speed, same path, same three hundred and sixty-five days. We'd freeze in the dark, sure. But we would not get pulled in.

Because gravity doesn't care what shape the mass is in. It only cares how much there is, and how far away you are. A black hole with the Sun's mass pulls on you with exactly the Sun's pull. Not one bit more.

So what is a black hole, then? Not a thing that sucks. A thing that's small.

Our Sun is about 1.4 million kilometers across. Squeeze all that mass down to a ball roughly six kilometers wide — a sphere you could walk across in an hour — and it becomes a black hole. Same mass. Just crushed.

And that's the whole trick. It's not that the pull got stronger. It's that you can now get *close*. With the real Sun, the surface stops you at 700,000 kilometers out. With the black hole, there's nothing to stop you. You can fall to within a few kilometers of all that mass, and down there, gravity is savage.

Think of it as a hole in a sidewalk. The hole isn't grabbing you. You just have to be standing right on top of it.

And if you did fall? You wouldn't feel yanked. You'd feel nothing at all. Free fall is weightless — same as an astronaut, floating, perfectly calm.

Until the difference kicks in. Gravity at your feet, a little stronger than at your head. Then a lot stronger. Then stronger than the bonds holding you together.

Nothing pulled you in. You fell. The universe just stopped catching you.
